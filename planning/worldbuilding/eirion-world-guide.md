# Eirion World Guide

Working guide to Eirion as a real world. Decisions are marked as decided. Everything else is a proposal for the author to accept, change, or cut.

## Decided (10/16/2026)

- **Eirion is a real world** with real human people. It is a different planet or dimension from Earth, with its own history and its own story apart from the Eternal Ones.
- **Technology is medieval:** bows, swords, shields, horses, castles. No gunpowder-and-magic setting.
- **No magic or fantasy creatures.** The series is historical fiction set in an afterlife, with light science fiction and adventure.
- **The Eternal Ones** are souls cast from Earth to Eirion. They do not age once they arrive. They live forever unless killed or unless they leave by another way, such as the Gate. See the [Codex entry](../../codex/lore/the-eternal-ones.md).
- **Gunpowder is banned by Alexander (decided 10/16/2026).** A native with a musket can kill an Eternal One, which would break the order his godhood depends on. He hunts down anyone who tries to make it. Napoleon quietly experimenting with it is a threat inside the Covenant. Scarce sulfur or saltpeter can back up the ban (optional).
- **The reference series are models for tone, politics, and war.** The author prefers the parts of those books that don't involve magic.
- **The natives of Eirion** are the world's own human population. Bray's eventual view is that the Eternal Ones should not interfere with them if it can be helped.

## What to take from the three reference series

From general knowledge of the books, not a formal study:

| Series | What to borrow | What to leave behind |
|---|---|---|
| *A Song of Ice and Fire* (George R.R. Martin) | Great houses with their own sigils and loyalties. Succession fights and politics that matter as much as battles. Regions modeled on real history, as Westeros draws on the Wars of the Roses. Several viewpoint characters on different sides. | Dragons, the Others, and the slow return of magic. |
| *The Wheel of Time* (Robert Jordan) | A large map with many nations and distinct cultures. Layers of history: ruins and legends of an earlier age that the current people only half understand. | The One Power, prophecy, and a chosen one. Magic is central to that series. |
| *The First Law* (Joe Abercrombie) | Grit. Morally gray characters. Realistic, ugly war, with mud, supply problems, and wounds that last. Cynical politics, and money as a form of power. | Its rare magic: the Magi and the Eaters. |

**The shared lesson:** these worlds feel real because their people have politics, economies, religions, and grudges that would exist even without the main characters. Eirion's natives need the same.

## The natives and the Eternal Ones (proposal)

Eternal Ones have been arriving for a long time. Native history would be shaped by them.

- **Some natives worship them as gods.** Beings who never age and remember other worlds would look divine. This could explain how Alexander's claim to godhood works in Eirion: his empire rests on native belief, not only on Eternal soldiers.
- **Some resent or fear them as invaders.** Their histories record Eternal conquerors who came before Alexander.
- **Some trade or work with them,** for their knowledge of medicine, engineering, and war.

This sets up a theme across all three books: Alexander builds an empire on top of native kingdoms, the Cohort fights alongside natives against that empire, and Bray ends the series believing the Eternal Ones should step aside and let Eirion's people write their own history.

### Already in the manuscript and Codex

- In Chapter 7, the wooden post bears two scripts: one familiar (French or Latin) and one Bray cannot place. The unplaceable script can be a native language.
- The creation myths are told by "the Ancients" and kept by "the Remnants of the Old Voice." These can become native religions and legends about where the Eternal Ones come from. Without magic, the myths are beliefs, not facts. That fits the Cohort ideal "Memory is not truth, only reflection."

### A native viewpoint character (optional)

A native noble, soldier, or commoner living under Covenant rule would give the natives a face, as Martin and Abercrombie use viewpoints on every side. It also gives Bray's non-interference choice at the end of Book 3 a person it protects.

## Open questions and pings

### 1. Some Codex details read as supernatural (needs a decision)

The premise itself (souls crossing worlds, never aging, a Gate to whatever lies beyond) can't be explained by Earth science. That's fine, but the Codex has other unexplained details:

- The Bastion's "memory-light" glowing from the stone.
- The Mirror Hall's glass showing "selves that could have been."
- The Loom, where souls are "pulled from" before arriving.

**Suggested rule:** the crossing is the only thing in Eirion that can't be explained. Arrival, agelessness, and the Gate are that mystery. Everything native to Eirion follows normal physical rules. Under that rule, either cut memory-light and the Mirror Hall, or tie them to the crossing as part of the mystery Alexander fears.

### 2. Other questions

- Should scarce sulfur or saltpeter back up the gunpowder ban?
- What does the Wane mean: a region, a season, or the in-between state of a new arrival?
- Does Eirion have a single sun and moon, a different sky, or a harsher atmosphere? Bray already notices that the air is harsh. That can be the light science fiction touch.
- What do natives call the Eternal Ones in their own languages?
- Why are Eternal Ones' firearms missing on arrival? Bray arrives in Chapter 6 with fatigues and boots but no rifle.
