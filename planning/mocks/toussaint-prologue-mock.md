# Chapter 2: The Roots of the Tree

### THE ARCHITECT

*"In overthrowing me, you have cut down in Saint-Domingue only the trunk of the tree of liberty. It will spring up again by the roots, for they are numerous and deep."*

Toussaint Louverture, 1802

Snow had been falling on the Jura since autumn, and the fortress had long ago stopped pretending to be warm.

The cell had one window, set so high and so deep in the wall that the light arrived already tired. Water crept down the stone through the day and froze there through the night. He had learned the hours by it. Wet meant noon. Ice meant it was time to sit closer to the fire.

The fire was smaller than it had been in the autumn. Everything was.

He was nearly sixty, by his own count. No one had written down the day he was born. They had written down what he was worth.

He had been a coachman once, at Bréda, and had known the horses better than the men who owned them. He had learned to read late, and slowly, and then all at once. He had learned the leaves, too: which ones broke a fever, which ones closed a wound, which ones only made a frightened man feel better, which was its own kind of medicine. Then he had learned war, and treaties, and the patience to sit across a table from Spain, from England, from France, and let each one believe it was the cleverest man in the room.

He had governed a colony that no longer belonged to its masters. He had written a constitution and named himself governor for life. At the time it had seemed the only wall high enough to keep the old masters out. Here, with nothing to do but think, he was less sure what he had been building, and for whom.

They had invited him to a meeting. A matter of the public good, the letter said. His safety assured. He had read the letter twice, and gone anyway, because a man who refuses every meeting is no longer governing. He is only hiding.

They took him at the door.

Since then he had written to the First Consul. Once as a general to a general. Once as a father. Once more, at length, setting down every order he had given and why, so that no clerk in Paris could say he had not explained himself.

No answer came. He had stopped expecting one somewhere around the second letter, and kept writing anyway. A record was a kind of answer, even if no one read it.

The commandant had gone down to the town for a few days. The guard who brought the bread did not meet his eyes.

He moved his chair closer to the hearth, and settled his coat around him, and put his head back against the stone. It was warm there, faintly, the way a horse's flank is warm.

He did not mean to sleep.

When the cold stopped, he noticed it the way a man notices a sound that has ended.

And then he heard leaves.

Not the dry rattle of the Jura beeches, stripped since October. Green leaves. Broad ones, moving the way they moved on the mountain above Bréda before the rain. He knew them by their sound. He knew the one for fever, the one for wounds.

There was no tree in the cell. There was no wind.

Someone was standing on the other side of the fire. He could not have said what they looked like. Only that they were waiting, the way he had once waited across a table, for the other side to speak first.

"The roots held," it said. "There is other ground."

He considered this. He had heard many offers in his life. He had learned that the first one is never the real one.

"On what terms?" he asked.

Something that might have been amusement.

"Your own."

*And in 1803, the cold did what an army could not.*

*But the roots ran deeper than the cell.*

*Six weeks later, an old lieutenant tore the white from the French flag and raised what was left.*

*His name was not written among the Uncrowned.*

*He would choose what to be called.*

## Notes for the author

### What the chapter does for Book 1

| Plant | Payoff |
|---|---|
| A cold cell, the unanswered letters to the First Consul | Ch 23 (current numbering): Napoleon holds him in a cold cell again and admits, "I read the first." Readers already felt those letters go unanswered. |
| "They had invited him to a meeting... His safety assured." He went anyway. | Ch 21: he goes to Napoleon's parley knowing the history. The prologue makes that choice readable instead of foolish. |
| "Governor for life," and his doubt about it | Ch 23: Napoleon's sharpest weapon, "You are me without the crown." Ch 32: his vote is a check on himself. |
| "On what terms?" | Who he is in one line: a negotiator to the end, even with the presence. Echoes the prologue pattern (the presence answers Shaw; it does not answer Bray). |
| "His name was not written among the Uncrowned. He would choose what to be called." | The Cohort's custom of titles, and the only prologue death that does not end among the Uncrowned. It tells readers there is more than one road in Eirion before Part 2 begins. |
| A flag with its white torn out | The book's title image. A banner broken on purpose to make a new one. |
| The leaves he knows by name | The presence's signature, felt through his own life. |

### How big is Toussaint's storyline?

**Decided 10/16/2026: about 8 chapters.** Toussaint has eight main chapters (8, 12, 16, 21, 23, 26, 29, 32). Bray has sixteen. See the outline's [structure section](../book-1-plot-outline.md#5-structure). If kept, this prologue chapter would be his ninth, and it costs about 700 words.

The prologue chapter works at this size because it does the setup the cut chapters used to do. Readers arrive at Ch 21 already knowing he walked into a trap like this once before.

### Facts to verify before use

- The epigraph wording and occasion: commonly given as spoken on boarding the ship that carried him to France, 1802.
- Bréda plantation; his work as a coachman and with horses; learning to read later in life.
- His knowledge of medicinal plants. Often reported; check the source.
- The arrest: invited to a meeting with General Brunet in June 1802 under assurances, then seized.
- Letters to Bonaparte from Fort de Joux, unanswered.
- The commandant's absence in the days before his death, and his being found dead in his chair by the hearth on 04/07/1803. Accounts vary; some details are legend.
- His age. His birth year is uncertain (about 1743). "Nearly sixty, by his own count" is written to cover that.
- The flag: Dessalines tearing the white from the French tricolor at Arcahaie, 05/18/1803, about six weeks after Toussaint's death. If the date or details don't hold, cut that line. The chapter works without it.

### If kept

- Insert as the new Chapter 2 (1803 falls between Hale in 1776 and Shaw in 1863). Every later chapter number shifts by one in the manuscript, README, chapter summaries, and the planning docs.
- Codex: his entry already says he died "imprisoned and left to die by the French Republic." In 1803 France was led by Bonaparte as First Consul, so the chapter says "First Consul," not "Emperor." He learns in Eirion that Napoleon crowned himself the next year, which can be a beat in Ch 8 or Ch 23.
