# Chapter 2: The Banner that Fell

### THE MORAL COMPASS

*"Freedom is never given; it is won. And won again. And then again. Until the winning of it becomes so natural that its possession is accepted without question." —* A. Philip Randolph

The ocean spray mixed with gunpowder smoke as shells burst along the beach. Behind him, six hundred men—former slaves, now freed men donning blue uniforms—advanced toward the earthen walls of the fortress. Their boots sunk into damp sand, each stride intentional under the weight of history being created through movement. The sand was soft beneath his boots. Too soft to hold the weight of what came next.

He was twenty-five, the youngest colonel in the Union Army. Boston-born, Harvard-educated, appointed to command the first all-Black regiment in the North. Now he led them against the walls of Fort Wagner, the Confederate stronghold guarding Charleston Harbor.

"Forward!" he shouted above the artillery's thunder, his sword catching the last light of day.

They had marched through the streets of Massachusetts, their boots striking the pavement with a defiant rhythm, while hostile crowds jeered and hurled slurs at their backs. They had trained relentlessly, cognizant that the shadow of execution awaited them if they were captured by the adversary. They had accepted half the pay of their white counterparts, a bitter pill to swallow, yet they stood resolute, their ranks unbroken, united by a shared purpose and unyielding spirit.

And now they charged a fortress deemed impregnable, their hearts pounding in sync with the relentless drumbeat of war. The air was thick with smoke and the acrid scent of gunpowder, each breath a testament to their unyielding courage. Shifting sand hampered their advance toward Fort Wagner's daunting walls.

The bullet struck his heart as he crested the parapet. He felt no pain—only the sudden absence of weight, as though gravity had forgotten him. Around him, his men continued forward, their bayonets flashing in the twilight.

As he fell, he saw one of his sergeants grasp the colors from his falling hand. The flag never touched the ground.

"Well done," he whispered, though no one could hear him above the battle.

The Confederates would strip his body and toss it into a mass grave with his Black soldiers—an intended humiliation that would become his greatest honor.

As darkness enveloped him, a strange lightness spread through his limbs. The sounds of battle receded, replaced by something like music—distant, beckoning.

And a presence—vast yet intimate—enfolded him:

"Your line held. But the battle continues elsewhere."

“You were meant for more than a monument.”

“I didn’t do enough,” the Moral Compass whispered.

“No. But you did what others would not. And that is why you will rise again.”

And the trench faded. The rifles silenced. The flag stilled.

And the Moral Compass breathed again.

*And in 1863, the rampart stood tall.*

*But it could not silence what climbed it.*

*He was buried not beneath his name, but among his brothers.*

*And now, he stands among the Uncrowned.*
