# Chapter 7 (Part 2, Chapter 2): Away From What I Left

##### **Scene 1: Departing the Shelter (2–3 pages)**

- Bray packs what he can—**Civil War canteen**, rucksack, scraps of old gear

- He **revisits the map**, trying to orient himself, and makes a **mental note of the path** etched in stone

- The silence feels more intentional now—**“not empty, but expectant”**

- He begins walking—no destination yet, just **movement to silence the guilt**

The fire was long gone, reduced to little more than a faint memory in the dark stone bowl of the cave. Peyton Bray sat with his back against the wall, wrapped in a stranger’s wool, staring out into the colorless plain where morning and night seemed to have no quarrel—just one long, dim truce of lightless hours.

The dream had rattled something loose in him. Tate’s face—boyish and earnest, lined with pain, framed in that yellow tent-glow—still hovered behind his eyes. He hadn’t dreamed of Tate, not since the first few months after. Not since he stopped attending the grief groups, stopped answering the calls from home. It had all felt too rehearsed. Everyone had the same words. They all wanted permission to stop asking.

But here, alone in this alien quiet, Tate’s voice had returned unprompted. Stronger than memory. Too vivid to ignore. Too raw to bury again.

Bray exhaled slowly and reached for the old canvas rucksack. The stitching had dried out around the corners, but it held. He packed what he could: the rusted Civil War-era canteen, a handful of dry moss he’d gathered the day before, the oilskin scrap. Then, nestled in a fold of the blanket he’d slept on, he saw it—**a** single dog tag, dull and cold, the chain broken short. The lettering was almost worn smooth, but the last name was still legible.

**CROMWELL.**

He hadn’t brought it with him. He was sure of that. And yet it lay there like it had always belonged—waiting.

Bray turned it over in his hand, thumb brushing the dent at the edge. The tag felt heavier than it should have. Not just in weight—but in presence. As if the memory had condensed into something solid.

He hesitated, then slipped it into the side pocket of the rucksack. It didn’t matter where it came from.

It was his now.

Before he left, he walked once more to the carved stone relief tucked near the overhang. The grooves in its surface were faint, like an idea almost forgotten—but they were there. A path. A structure. An implication that all this desolation had been *witnessed* before.

He didn’t know if it led anywhere. But staying here was no longer an option.

He tightened the straps of the rucksack and stepped beyond the lip of the shelter. The wind was low, the air dry enough to grate the inside of his nostrils. The fine grey grit shifted beneath his boots as he descended from the outcrop. No trail, no compass. Just a horizon he couldn’t read and a sky that refused to change.

The world whispered in stillness.

He walked without aim for what felt like hours. The ground changed beneath him—sometimes soft, sometimes like packed clay, sometimes shot through with shards of dark stone that cracked underfoot with the brittle crunch of antlers. He tracked the sunless light like a sailor follows current, making mental adjustments each time the terrain forced him to veer.

Every so often, he passed something that caught his eye. Not landmarks—nothing that purposeful—but interruptions. A strip of fabric twisted around a spike of rock. A dented metal bowl half-swallowed by sediment. Once, a skeletal ribcage—the size and shape of a man’s—picked clean and bleached near-white, with no trace of skull or limbs. Bray gave it a wide berth.

He felt no need to be brave out here.

By midday—though he only guessed the time by the ache in his calves and the dryness of his mouth—he found a small ridge dotted with black moss and gathered beneath it. It wasn’t shade. There was no real shadow in a place without sun. But it was shelter enough to stop, to breathe, to let the weight of movement bleed off.

He sat down heavily, pulled the canteen from his pack, and took a slow sip. The water inside tasted faintly metallic, filtered by time and stone, but it was wet and it stayed down. He rinsed his mouth and let the grit spill out beside him.

Then, reflexively, he reached into the side pocket of the rucksack.

The dog tag.

He drew it out slowly, the short length of broken chain clinking faintly against the metal. The name—**CROMWELL.**—was still there, faded and dulled by time, but intact. He ran his thumb over the lettering, tracing each groove like it might speak back to him.

Tate had worn it every day, even when regulation said otherwise. Said it kept him grounded, reminded him who he was. Bray used to tease him for it. “You gonna hang that thing on your rearview when we’re home?” he’d joked once, and Tate had just smiled, like he already knew they wouldn’t be.

Bray could still hear his voice—light, cracked with fatigue, still trying to be the funny one even when the pain flared in his side.

> “You always worry too much, Moose. That’s your problem.”

He held the tag a moment longer, the metal cool against his skin, then folded it gently into a scrap of cloth and returned it to the pack.

The ridge offered a higher vantage than most of the terrain he’d passed. Beyond the basin below, the ground curved in long, sweeping arcs—natural, at first glance, but as he studied the lines, he saw that some of the angles were too clean, too intentional. Like foundations buried long ago.

He made a slow circuit around the ridge, scanning the dirt for anything familiar—boots, tools, even footprints. Instead, he found a length of rope, thin and dry, frayed at the ends and looped twice around a stake hammered into the stone. Nothing attached to it. No camp, no structure, no corpse.

But the knot was tight. A sailor’s knot. Fresh when it had been tied. He ran his fingers over it. The rope twitched slightly in the breeze—then stilled.

Nearby, under a shallow overhang, Bray found what might have once been a firepit—just a circle of blackened stones, charred through to the core. In the center sat a single item: a metal spoon bent nearly in half.

Someone else had sat here.

Someone else had eaten here.

And someone else had *moved on.*

Bray crouched and picked up the spoon. The metal flaked slightly, rust falling like scabs. He pocketed it anyway.

A few feet away, something else glinted in the dirt. He knelt and brushed the grit aside carefully.

A set of initials, scratched into stone.

> J.L.

No dates. No rank. Just the two letters, scored hard and deep—like someone needed to leave *something* behind, even if it was just proof they had existed.

Bray stood, rubbing his sore knees. The horizon was still empty. The wind whispered up the ridge in short bursts, rising and dying like breath.

He looked out across the plain again, eyes narrowing. In the distance, beyond the sweep of rock and grey, something **broke the horizon**.

A shape. Low. Jagged. Not natural.

Not close, either.

But *there.*

He didn’t know if it was the place from the stone map. He didn’t know if it was shelter or trap or something older than either. But it was a destination. And for now, that was enough.

Bray checked the laces on his boots, slung the pack tighter against his shoulders, and began to walk.

##### **Scene 2: Signs in the Landscape (4–6 pages)**

- The terrain becomes stranger—**not alien, but subtly wrong**:

  - Trees stunted or twisted, like they were grown in windless air

  - Fossilized roots above ground

  - Remnants of **non-contemporaneous debris**: a rusted helmet next to colonial boots, ash-drifted photos fused to bark

- Bray reflects on **Tate’s injury**, the opium, and what he never said

- He finds **a shallow grave**, unmarked, disturbed. Inside: only dog tags. Not Tate’s, but too similar to ignore.

The landscape no longer unfurled like a wasteland—it bent and bowed, subtle and silent, as though it remembered what it had once been and still mourned it.

Bray moved with caution, his boots scraping against a hardened crust of stone laced with finer veins of granular mineral—almost like volcanic glass, but duller, with no luster. The world around him was not dead, not lifeless—it was something worse. It was paused, like a theater before the next act, its cast missing, the curtains drawn but not fallen.

The earth bore signs of passing—not movement, but memory. Shallow impressions underfoot didn’t read like footsteps but more like the scars of weight, something massive having long ago pressed down and not returned. The indentations followed no logical trail. They twisted and arced, faded and picked up again as if the land itself were trying to remember how to be walked upon.

He passed through a stretch of land where the grey dust was heaped high in eddies, like snowdrifts made of ash. Between these mounds, bone-pale stones jutted upward, each one leaning slightly, as though cowering against the wind—even though no wind blew. Bray reached out and touched one. It was cold, colder than stone should be, and smooth on one face—almost polished.

There was a mark on it. A long groove, straight but uneven in depth, like someone had tried to strike it with a blade and failed to follow through. It wasn’t a message. Just frustration fossilized.

Bray moved on.

He stopped next at what might once have been a campsite. The remnants were skeletal: two rusted tent poles, half-driven into a bed of crumbling gravel; a boot print hardened into mud that had since turned to stone. The ground here had a different texture—grit gave way to compressed ash, flat and slightly springy, like it had once burned hot enough to melt the edges of everything left behind.

There was a burned circle in the middle, rimmed with blackened rock, and the faint suggestion of a tarp edge buried beneath one side. Bray knelt and peeled the layers of ash back carefully, revealing a strip of canvas, stenciled with numbers he couldn’t decipher—military, maybe, but from no uniform he recognized.

On instinct, he checked his surroundings. No motion. Just stillness. The kind that felt watched, even if the eyes had long since closed.

Something about the scene rang false—not in the way it was placed, but in what was missing. There were no bones. No remains. No markings. Whoever had camped here had vanished deliberately or entirely.

Bray stood and moved toward a rise ahead.

As he climbed the slope—his legs dragging now, his shoulders burning under the weight of the rucksack—he began to feel it again: the pressure of memory without clarity.

Tate's voice returned like a tune hummed in another room. Not sharp, not piercing, just persistent.

"You always worry too much, Moose. That’s your problem."

The sound brought with it a memory—not from Vietnam, but long before that. An old football field, brown with drought and cleated mud. Bray and Tate in practice gear, helmets off, Tate laughing as Bray wiped blood from his lip.

"You gotta lower your shoulder, not your head. You’re not a battering ram. You’re a damn avalanche."

He smiled faintly despite himself.

Then the memory folded in on itself, as if it had run out of detail.

The ground leveled. The wind shifted.

He looked up.

A tall wooden post jutted out from a stone ledge ahead, so weathered it had begun to splinter into long, dry threads at its base. No banner hung from it. No message was nailed to it. But the post was clearly placed—driven hard into stone with a purpose.

Bray approached.

Carved into the upper half were two distinct sets of markings. The first was looping, delicate, as if written by someone with ceremonial intent. The second—scrawled in shorter, deeper strokes—was cruder, desperate, almost angry.

But it was the phrase carved into the middle that stopped him cold.

> "Beware the voice that promises order without mercy. For even Lucifer claimed dominion before he fell."

The words weren’t ancient. They were deliberate. Sharpened. Not a scripture Bray recognized verbatim, but rooted in a language he understood—faith, distortion, tyranny cloaked as clarity.

He didn’t move for a long time.

The silence returned, but now it wasn’t empty. It was listening. A world waiting to see if he would accept what it had offered—or question it further.

He touched the carving once, then stepped back. Whatever had written it wasn’t trying to be clever.

They were trying to warn.

He walked on.

The words carved into the post still gnawed at him long after he left it behind. His boots dragged in the grey soil; his legs moved out of stubbornness, not strength. But his mind—his mind was no longer here.

It was back in Georgia, seventeen years old, sitting with Tate Cromwell on the broken fence behind the high school field, both of them holding thin, rattling letters stamped with the seal of the United States Army.

Neither of them said anything at first. It had been Tate who finally broke the silence.

"We stay together, right, Moose?"

Bray had nodded without thinking, his throat dry.

"Yeah. Always."

Tate had grinned, that boyish, lopsided grin that never fully left his face even when the years hardened the rest of him.

"Neither of us walks outta this unless we're walkin' together."

Vietnam didn’t break that pact. Not immediately. Not even when Tate took a bullet in the first month—an ambush, a trap he hadn't seen fast enough. Bray still remembered the sick thud of Tate's body hitting the ground, remembered the frantic pressure he applied to Tate's ribs, the screaming for a medic that didn’t come fast enough.

The doctors at base camp said Tate could take a medical discharge, go home, get patched up properly.

Tate refused.

Because Bray was still in-country.

Because they had made a promise.

Bray hadn’t argued. Not hard enough. He saw the weight in Tate’s eyes, the pain twisting every step he took afterward, the stiffness of his laughter. He knew about the opium—the quiet deals, the folded packets of powder carried between fingers too calloused to shake.

He had told himself it wasn’t his place to intervene. That it would pass. That the pain would ease.

But deep down, Bray knew the truth.

He didn’t want Tate to leave.

He didn’t want to be alone.

It wasn’t the bullet that killed Tate Cromwell. It was the slow erosion of the man he had been.

When the Viet Cong struck their camp under cover of monsoon rain, Tate didn’t wake fast enough. Didn’t move fast enough. Bray had fought his way to Tate’s side, firing wildly into the green-black tangle of trees, dragging Tate upright by the collar.

He didn’t realize at first that Tate was already gone.

Didn’t realize he was carrying a corpse through mud and fire and screaming until the platoon regrouped a mile out, and the medics pulled Tate from his arms with grim, efficient hands.

The body bag was perfunctory.

The silence afterward wasn’t.

Bray stayed.

Through three more tours.

Not because he loved the war. Not because he believed in it anymore.

But because some promises are a noose you tie yourself.

Victory or death. There would be no middle road for Peyton Bray.

The landscape around him blurred and sharpened, blurred and sharpened again, as though even Eirion struggled to decide whether he belonged here. His breath rasped in his throat. The ache in his chest never left.

He stumbled upon it by accident—a shallow dip in the soil, disturbed by wind and erosion. At first he thought it was just another hollow.

Then he saw the glint of metal.

He knelt, brushing aside the fine, grey dust with numb fingers.

A dog tag.

Singular.

Worn so smooth the name was unreadable. Half of the chain still looped through a brittle scrap of canvas. No bones. No gear. Just the tag, resting alone in the half-filled grave.

He stared at it for a long time, unable to touch it, unwilling to bury it deeper.

It wasn’t Tate’s.

But it was close enough.

Close enough to remind him that whatever waited ahead—fortress, bastion, or graveyard—it wasn't built for heroes.

It was built for the unfinished.

He rose slowly, the rucksack creaking against his back, and turned his face once more toward the distant shape clawing at the horizon.

He began to walk again.

And this time, he did not look back.

##### **Scene 3: Hunger and Memory (5–7 pages)**

- Bray begins to weaken—hunger now impossible to ignore

- Flashback memory: *Bray talking with his dream colleges—University of Tennessee and Wake Forest University—about playing college football on scholarship.* His parents telling him how proud they were of his potential for a free college education.

- As hunger peaks, Bray **discovers edible moss** growing in the cracks of a stone cairn. Carefully tests it—tastes foul but keeps it down

- Next to the moss is a **folded journal page**, weighted by a bayonet blade. Words half-faded:

> *“A journey awaits, but I am not sure if I have the strength to pursue one more grand adventure in search of absolution. Can I live with what I have done, what I can still change?*
>
> *C.G.”*

He was starving.

It crept in slowly, not as a pang but as a tightening coil—something wrapping around his torso and tugging with each step. The ache behind his ribs wasn’t sharp, but *settled*, like a hand pressing gently, persistently, reminding him that time moved forward here, even if the sky refused to change.

The wind had picked up again, soft and bitter. Not enough to chill him, but enough to whistle low through the jagged stone hills, winding over old fractures in the earth that didn’t seem natural. His breath had begun to labor. Not because of exertion—he’d marched farther in worse terrain before—but because something in this place leeched away strength. Not violently. Just enough to make every motion second-guessed.

He focused on his feet. One step. Then the next. He followed the map—at least the part he remembered. The scratched path leading to the lone symbol etched into the stone back near the cave. A bastion, a fortress—whatever it was, it was the only shape in this world that suggested someone had once tried to build something against the endless grey.

But now, his thoughts began to wander, not out of reflection but *necessity*. Hunger blurred his vision, but it also pulled at something deeper. He remembered grass. Bright green, smooth underfoot. Not the fibrous ash-cracked weeds of this place, but real grass—a practice field.

The sun had been high that day, burning down on the orange foam tackling dummies and the chalked sidelines of his high school stadium. Bray remembered the whistle, the bark of the assistant coach, the sweat running from under his helmet down the back of his neck.

And then—out of nowhere—the admissions rep from the University of Tennessee, arms crossed, standing just beyond the fence.

> “Son,” the man had said after practice, “you’ve got legs like a plow horse and a first-step like a cheetah. You want to play ball in Knoxville?”

It wasn’t the first time he’d heard it. Wake Forest had reached out the week before. A smaller school, but a quieter campus. More academic prestige. His mother had beamed when he told her.

> “They said you might even qualify for academic aid on top of the sports money,” she’d said, blinking back tears as she scrubbed her hands in the kitchen sink.
>
> “A free ride, Moose. You won’t owe a thing.”

And his father—stoic, never effusive—had just nodded and offered a rough clap on the shoulder.

> “Always knew you’d make something of yourself.”

But none of that had come to pass. Not after the draft notice.

His stomach growled—an audible snarl now. The memory folded back into silence.

He crested another low hill and paused. Ahead, scattered like broken teeth, were a series of cairns. Stones stacked not high, but tight—intentional. Grave markers, maybe. Trail symbols. He couldn’t tell. But he followed them, weaving slowly between them until he saw something different.

A crack in the stone, where two slate-colored boulders leaned against each other, and something green pushed through. Not the sickly ash-brushed vegetation he’d passed earlier. This was deep green, the color of moss at the base of a real tree, moist and veined.

He crouched, eyes narrowing.

It looked edible. It looked *alive*.

He scraped a small patch from the crevice and sniffed. It smelled damp. Earthy. Rotting and vital all at once. His gut twisted at the idea, but he tore a small piece away and chewed.

It was bitter. Unforgiving. Like algae scraped from the inside of a drainpipe.

But it stayed down.

He swallowed slowly, then ate more—sparingly, but enough to dull the tightness in his belly. He knelt there, resting briefly against the stone, when he saw it:

A folded sheet of paper, tucked just beneath a smooth stone at the base of the cairn.

The weight pinning it down was a bayonet blade, rusted, handle missing. It looked ceremonial—planted, not dropped.

Bray reached down and gently pulled the paper free.

The ink had blurred in places. The parchment was stiff, stained by time and exposure. But he could read enough.

> *“A journey awaits, but I am not sure if I have the strength to pursue one more grand adventure in search of absolution. Can I live with what I have done? Can I rest knowing I can still do good?”*

It was signed, simply: C.G.

He read it twice.

The words didn't comfort him. But they didn’t frighten him either. They sounded like something he might have written—if he were braver. If he were honest enough to admit what still lived behind his own eyes.

He folded the note and returned it to its place beneath the blade.

The moss still clung to his teeth.

And ahead, the ground began to slope downward—toward something darker, heavier, *constructed*.

He rose, wiped his hands against his thighs, and moved forward.

##### **Scene 4: First Distant Sign of Others (4–5 pages)**

- Bray hears sound—**not a voice**, but the distant *thunk* of something large moving

- He doesn’t investigate immediately, but it changes his path—he follows the etched map’s rough direction

- Finds an old **wooden post driven into the stone**—paint flaked off, symbols carved in two scripts (one familiar—French or Latin, one unplaceable)

- The wind returns here for the first time. It howls low, *almost like breath over a bottle’s mouth*

##### **Scene 5: Emotional Cliffhanger (2–3 pages)**

- Bray rests near a half-buried trail marker—half Union flag, half something he doesn’t recognize

- He begins writing in a torn field manual using charcoal from his last fire

- Final line:

> *“I don’t know if I’m walking toward something or just away from what I left behind.”*

- Then, in the distance—**movement**. A lone figure. Still. Watching. Not approaching.
