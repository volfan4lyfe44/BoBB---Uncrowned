# Chapter 6 (Part 2, Chapter 1): The Grey Waking

His eyes fluttered open, blinking away the fog of unconsciousness. It took him a moment to realize he wasn't dead, but he couldn't say where he was or how he got there. He tried to sit up, but a wave of dizziness forced him back onto what felt like coarse, gritty soil.

"What the hell..." he muttered, his voice hoarse and unfamiliar.

His right hand swept the ground beside him for his rifle. It found only grit. He tried the other side and found nothing there either. For months that weapon had never been more than an arm’s length away, and its absence felt like a missing limb.

The shot. He shoved a hand up under his shirt and twisted to reach high on his back, below the right shoulder blade, where it had taken him. Whole skin. No hole, no blood, no scar. He pressed harder, as if the pain might be hiding underneath. Something about where it had landed nagged at him, a detail just out of reach, and he pushed it away.

His fingers went to his throat out of habit and found the chain, the tags cold against his chest. Still there. He let them drop back under his shirt.

And the quiet. No cicadas. No birdcalls. No leaves stirring overhead. After months of the jungle’s endless noise, the silence rang in his ears.

As his eyes adjusted to the weak light filtering through a perpetually overcast sky, he saw no lush jungle canopy overhead, no cotton-ball clouds floating leisurely across a serene blue sky. Instead, the air was biting and harsh, redolent with a strange scent—dry, stale, and metallic, like rust. Bray found himself surrounded by a vast expanse of grey particles, not dust or ash, but something distinct, almost crystalline in the way the light refracted off it. It crunched faintly under his boots as he rose to his feet, causing the peculiar substance to swirl around momentarily before settling back onto the flat, featureless ground beneath him. He brushed at his fatigues, finding the fabric stiff and oddly brittle. The chill seeped into his bones, a deep, penetrating cold that had nothing to do with the temperature.

"Where am I?" Bray muttered to himself, looking around and trying to get his bearings. He scanned the horizon, but saw nothing but the strange, grey substance, stretching on without end. No trees, no hills, no sign of anything remotely familiar. Nothing within sight seemed *human*.

*This place*, he thought, *wherever it is, is not any place I've heard of on Earth.* His memories of basic training, hazy as they were, began to focus. Direction, shelter, food, water. The four necessities. The bare minimum for survival in all this nothingness. He looked up at the sky, a bleak, grey canvas with a diffused light that might have been clouds, fog, or something far more alien. No sun pierced through the haze.

Bray clenched his jaw, forcing down the sensation of unreality. He hadn't even dared laugh—the kind of barking laugh his father let loose while making brisket, or his own boisterous laugh when hauling in a last-second winning catch in high school. The echoes of his past felt like ghosts here.

He took a deep breath, the grey, metallic air rasping his lungs. Survival instincts, honed in the jungles of Vietnam, kicked in. The sheer emptiness of this place, its vast, mocking silence, was almost as unnerving as the chaotic roar of the Viet Cong firefights he’d only just left. He scanned the horizon, seeking a landmark, any sign of life, anything to anchor him to reality.

"Damn it, Bray, think!"

His own voice, gruff and tight, sounded foreign in the oppressive stillness. He needed a starting point. A landmark, a fixed point in this strange new world. He studied the grey light filtering down, trying to decipher if it was truly a directional indicator. If it moved the way the sun did, rising and setting, it could tell him east from west… but the silence around him was a living thing, swallowing any sound. Was there a pattern in the light, a flicker or a movement that could point him somewhere?

His ears caught a snatch of a familiar voice.

"You’re too slow, Moose. C’mon, light on your feet…”

The voice of Coach Johnson, gravelly and impatient, echoed through his mind. The taste of victory after a ferocious finish to a football game flashed before his eyes: the roar of the crowd, the smell of freshly cut grass, the thud of the pigskin in his massive hands. The pain of that final push, the agonizing snaps of his hamstring, the endless hours of physical therapy – all the memories of his high school years, a fading dream now.

The metallic tang of the strange air twisted in Bray’s gut, pulling him from the memory.

*Moose*.

The nickname had a life of its own, a resonance that echoed even through this alien landscape. It followed him everywhere, from the roiling jungles of Vietnam to this sterile emptiness. It seemed to cling to him like a shroud, a brand he couldn't scrub off, a past that wouldn't let go.

He saw himself again as a skinny, awkward, gangly kid, all elbows and knees, his thick, black beard a controversial statement in seventh grade.

“How old are you really? Aren't you a little too old to be in middle school?" teased a kid named Ryan, as young Peyton lumbered his way through Mr. Smith's homeroom. The room erupted in laughter, even the girls giggled behind their folded hands. Peyton had shrugged it off then, but the nickname, like a burr clinging to his jeans, stuck.

Then, high school hit. The lanky boy transformed into a powerful young man. Every muscle gained definition, his beard grew darker and thicker, a testament to his developing masculinity. He filled out that frame as if it were his only purpose. He still ran… a little clunky, a little ungainly, more of a lope than a stride. Like a young moose trying to clear a fence.

The nickname, though, had firmly established itself. It seemed to suit him impeccably. "Peyton" was barely a sound he acknowledged anymore, except from his parents. "Moose" had evolved into something more profound than just a name; it had become his identity.

Coach Johnson, a rough-and-tumble linebacker in his college days, had never seen anyone move like him.

"He runs like a damn Moose," the coach barked down the sideline, "but he's got heart that could drive a tank!"

His memory flickered again, pulling him back to that monstrous grey landscape. He was a creature out of place, all that strength trapped in an endless void.

Bray was pulled from his thoughts by another voice. It was a voice that demanded his focus, one that brooked no delay.

"Bray? ...Don't you dare get weak now, you hear? Soldiers don't back down from anything except enemy fire."

He almost laughed. The Sergeant's face, weathered and stern, swirled in his mind, bearing the grime of countless military drills. He could almost hear the barked commands echoing in the air. His heart throbbed, a drumbeat of old memories and present uncertainties.

The gruff voice morphed into another, colder, more sinister.

"Hit whatever you see, soldier. Let them learn a lesson they'll never forget." The voice of Captain Thompson, his face a mask of grim determination.

Each sliver of memory, separated in time and space, was now interwoven in the fabric of his fragmented reality. Was this his sanity breaking down? Or were these glimpses into his past a means of communication in this new reality?

"Bray!"

The voice, a low, easy drawl in the back of his mind, sent a shiver down his spine. A voice familiar to him, one he knew and trusted. Not his own, but that of a . . . friend, teammate, or fellow soldier? "No time for reminiscin', Moose," the voice said, stretching the nickname out the way only one person ever had, half joke, half dare. "We got a job to do." Bray listened. His memories lay tangled like tape spilling out of an 8-track chewed up by a malfunctioning car stereo.

Bray didn't hesitate. He chose a direction, a sliver of light dancing on the endless grey. It was a flicker, barely perceivable, but it was enough. In the gaping abyss of this new reality, a beacon, however faint, was better than nothing.

He began to walk.

The grey whispered beneath his boots, crumbling like dried powder. Hours bled into each other, marked only by the subtle shift of the light, the increasing weariness in his legs, the tightening ache in his chest that he couldn’t quite place. The silence pressed on him, a suffocating pressure that he found himself both yearning for and fleeing from.

A small pang of guilt tugged at his chest, of a friend lost in Vietnam. A pain too heavy to handle. Would his fellow soldiers lost to history have done this? Kept walking? Or would they have just slumped in the shadows, numb, lost in the endless fog of pain and regret?

Bray couldn’t say. He swallowed hard, shaking the image from his head. Sleep was a luxury he couldn’t afford, not yet. Not in this desolate wasteland.

When his legs finally gave out, he lay down on the open grey with no shelter and no fire, arms folded tight across his chest against the cold. He tried to follow the instructions echoing in his head like an old, forgotten song, but the silence, the endless emptiness, pressed in on him. The unnatural quiet held an unsettling weight, a reminder of the quiet before the storm.

Sleep came in fitful bursts, plagued by nightmares. Bright light, the sickening thud of impact, the gruff voice of his commanding officer screaming in his ear. He woke with a gasp, his heart hammering against his ribs.

"It wasn't supposed to end like that," he whispered into the silent air. "Not like that."

\* \* \*

He didn’t sleep again. When the light had shifted enough to call it morning, he was stiff and cold on the open ground, grit worked into every crease of his fatigues. Bray rose with a grunt. His joints popped—whether from tension or cold, he couldn’t tell.

He needed to move again.

The terrain had begun to subtly change as he walked on. The crystalline particulate gave way to something denser—darker, packed in sheets that cracked underfoot like dried clay. Shallow gullies cut the surface at odd intervals, as if water had once carved paths here but had long since evaporated, leaving behind only the memory of movement.

The light still came from nowhere in particular. No sun. No moon. Just that diffuse, off-white glow that seemed to hover evenly above the world, as if the sky were a dome of weathered bone.

Bray trudged forward, adjusting the strip of cloth he’d tied around one hand for warmth. The silence was so absolute, his footfalls sounded like gunshots. But that wasn’t what bothered him most.

It was the feeling of being watched—not from above, but from underneath.

As if the land itself were listening.

A few hours—maybe more—passed. Time here was unreliable. He stopped measuring it. Thirst and hunger gnawed at him, dull and persistent. But worse than hunger was the absence of scent. No rot. No grass. No sweat. Just the faint metallic tang, and the faint static dryness that clung to everything.

Ahead, the terrain buckled.

Ridges. Shallow ones, not more than shoulder height, but enough to break the horizon’s monotony. Bray made for them, heart kicking into something like hope. Terrain meant shelter. Shelter meant survival.

He crested the first ridge and stopped cold.

Laid out below was a shallow basin—maybe a dry lakebed—scored with geometric scars. Not natural erosion. Not farming. Almost like someone had tried to plow the earth into symbols and failed, or been interrupted mid-sentence. Some of the lines curled into spirals, others cut jagged and sharp. All were filled with that same dull, grey sediment—but nothing grew in them.

It felt like standing on a forgotten map.

He crouched and touched one of the grooves. The dirt crumbled easily, like dead skin. No heat. No cold. Just... absence.

A flash of memory rose unbidden: a hand gripping his shoulder. *“You can’t save everybody, Bray. Sometimes the jungle wins.”*

He exhaled through his nose. Wiped the sweat that wasn’t there. And moved on.

The basin curved inward gradually, the land forming a natural bowl. At the far end, tucked between two sloping ridges, Bray spotted a rise of stone—a flat outcropping capped by what looked like a crude overhang. Shelter. His legs ached, but the sight of it injected just enough energy to push through.

The climb wasn’t difficult. Just steep enough to force him onto hands and knees in the final stretch. The stone was warm, dry, and scored with strange striations—as if dragged by glacial time or tools meant for something other than war. When he reached the shelf and pulled himself up, the wind caught him—cooler here, cleaner. The shelter itself was no more than a shallow cave, but it was dry and offered a defensible view of the basin below.

Bray stepped inside and paused. His boot crunched on something.

He looked down.

A shard of clay, dull brown with a rim worn smooth. Next to it, buried under a thin layer of dust, was the rest of it—a broken Roman-style amphora, its long neck snapped cleanly. Nearby, a set of leather sandals, brittle and warped with age, rested side by side as if someone had taken them off for the night and simply never returned.

He crouched. Inspected the sandals. The stitching was still visible—simple, careful work. Not rotted. Preserved by dryness.

He scanned the rest of the shelter and found more oddities: a rusted metal canteen, stamped with a pattern of concentric rings. A canvas rucksack, half-buried under a pile of gravel, still intact enough to tug loose. The canvas was stiff and cracked, but the stitching held.

Inside:

- A spool of linen thread

- A rusted spoon

- A folded scrap of oilskin

- And a note—so faded it was almost illegible. Just two words:

> *“Keep walking.”*

Bray stuffed the note back inside and slung the bag over his shoulder.

In the corner of the shelter, draped over what might have once been a firepit, a tricorn hat rested atop a bundle of rotting wool. It looked absurd—like it had been plucked off a Revolutionary War reenactor. But the cloth was real. The stitching authentic. He picked it up and turned it over. The brim cracked under his grip, but held.

He smirked, couldn’t help himself.

“Hell of a fashion statement,” he muttered, settling the thing on his head.

The absurdity made it easier to breathe.

At the back of the cave, nestled against the wall, a thin trickle of condensation dripped down into a shallow pool. Bray dropped to his knees, cupped his hands, and drank. It was cool. Clean. A luxury he hadn’t dared hope for. He drank again, then held the old canteen under the drip. It filled slowly, a mouthful at a time, but it was something.

From the edge of the stone shelf, he spotted a cluster of low shrubs, scraggly and greyish, growing along a fracture in the stone slope. He didn’t trust them immediately—too many toxic plants in Vietnam had taught him caution—but the leaves didn’t burn to the touch. He pocketed a few, resolving to test them later.

He built the fire small, from brittle kindling and a dry root system pulled from the rock wall, and lit it with the Zippo from his breast pocket. The brass was worn smooth where his thumb had rubbed it through two tours, and it caught on the second strike. He sat back against the stone. The light danced across the cave. It made the discarded artifacts look less like relics and more like ghosts, frozen in time and abandonment.

The firelight reached the back wall of the cave and caught on something: a smooth, flat surface carved into the stone. Strange symbols, etched into the rock, sent a shiver down his spine.

He approached cautiously, running his fingers over the stone. It felt ancient, worn smooth by the passage of time. As he traced the curves of the inscription, he realized it was not a language he recognized.

Then he saw it. Familiar. Not a language, but an articulation.

"This place," the inscription read, "is no victory. It is not the peace I sought, nor the end I fought for. We bled and died, all for what? For another master, for another tyrant, clothed in different silks, wielding a different sword?"

Bray knew that kind of hand, the blunt passion of it. This wasn't the languid flourish of a scholar, but a mark made in anger, in grief, by a man driven to extremes.

"Crassus is dead, but Rome lives on," the inscription continued. "Slavery endures, masked by gilded words and hollow promises. This—" The words broke off, then resumed, cut deeper. "This will be my eternal vigil. A monument to the failures of men."

Bray touched the inscription again, its jagged angles pressing into his palm. He thought of the flask he used to carry in his rucksack, and how many nights it had gotten him through. A breeze, carrying the bitter tang of this forsaken world, whispered around him. The jungle in Vietnam had been denser, alive, buzzing with insects and the insistent calls of unseen creatures. The silence here was a different kind of beast, staggering in its vastness and emptiness.

But there was something here. Something buried beneath the sterile surface. He traced the inscription again, letting his fingers wander over the worn surface. It confirmed what his gut told him: he wasn't alone. Someone had been here before him. Someone who, like him, wandered the blank canvas of this place, searching for something.

He turned back to the artifacts and stared at them for a long while.

Not one era. Not one people. A Roman vessel. A colonial hat. A Civil War soldier’s gear.

*This place doesn’t care* when *you’re from*, he realized. *Only that your story wasn’t finished.*

The thought chilled him more than the wind.

Outside, the grey plain stretched on forever. But in here, surrounded by the scattered remnants of other lives—some long lost, some barely faded—he felt the first hint of something resembling peace.

Not safety. But peace.

For now.

\* \* \*

The fire had dimmed to a quiet glow. Shadows clung to the corners of the cave, and Bray, wrapped in the coarse wool of a long-dead soldier’s blanket, finally surrendered to sleep.

And the jungle returned.

He heard it before he saw it: the buzz of cicadas, the distant thump of a generator, the rhythmic flap of wet canvas in the monsoon breeze. He was inside the tent again—canvas overhead, lantern swinging from the crossbar, the smell of mildew and cheap tobacco.

Someone was laughing.

He turned. Tate Cromwell was in the tent with him, wearing sweat-drenched Army fatigues that had once fit but now seemed too loose, sleeves rolled up, still wearing that dog tag he always pretended not to care about. Tate crouched low over an overturned crate, dealing out cards with one hand, the other favoring his side. His laugh was easy, warm—almost normal.

“I’m still sharp enough to clean you out, Moose. You better fold now and save face.”

Bray smirked, fanned out his cards. Junk. A mix of diamonds and low clubs. He didn't care. Tate’s grin reminded him of high school—a smaller, wiry kid in a No. 3 jersey, always outrunning the corners, always quick to flash that same crooked grin when they shared a touchdown.

But the smile faded too quickly this time. Tate winced and pressed his hand against his ribs.

Bray’s own smirk faded.

“Still hurting?” he asked.

Tate nodded. “Yeah. Ever since Phu Bai. Doc says the shrapnel probably nicked something deep. Can’t even lie flat without it lighting me up.”

Bray leaned forward. “You should rest. Lay off the cards. Or go to the medic again.”

Tate just chuckled, though it sounded dry.

“You know how many times I’ve been back? Guy in supply keeps getting me more of that stuff from Cambodia. I take it easy when I can… but the pain’s not going anywhere.”

Bray didn’t respond immediately. He scratched the back of his neck. The heat inside the tent seemed to thicken.

“Opium’s not a solution, Tate.”

Tate raised an eyebrow, and his eyes went to the flask by Bray’s elbow. “It’s not a solution, no. But it’s the only thing that works. You think I want to lean on it?”

Bray looked away. “I didn’t say that.”

“No, you didn’t.” Tate’s voice softened. “But I know that look.”

There was silence for a beat. The cards between them faded into abstraction.

Tate sighed and stood slowly, wincing again as he straightened up. He nodded toward the exit flap of the tent.

“I’m gonna go lie down. Ease up a bit.”

Bray didn’t stop him. He just nodded once, stiffly.

“Yeah. Alright.”

Tate turned, paused at the edge of the canvas flap. The lantern light flickered behind him, casting his shadow long across the floor.

“You’d do the same if it was you, Moose.”

And then he was gone.

He jerked awake.

The fire had gone out. Only smoke curled upward from the charred remains of the tinder. His chest was tight, shirt damp with sweat.

His hands still trembled.

He stared into the grey nothing outside the cave. The world had not changed. It was still dust and ruin. But the dream had left a residue—something sticky in his bones, something unfinished.

He rubbed his face with both hands.

“Tate,” he whispered. The name came unbidden, like a muscle memory. He hadn’t said it aloud in years. Not even at the funeral he never made it home for.

He didn’t remember standing up, but he was already at the cave’s edge, staring out at the colorless horizon. A part of him hoped to see something—a figure, maybe, a shape from the memory.

But there was only grey.

The silence pressed in again.

This time, it felt like guilt.

\* \* \*

The dream still clung to his chest as Bray stepped back out into the open grey. The cold had teeth now—not the kind that bit into the skin, but the kind that *sank into the marrow*, slow and deliberate, as if trying to settle inside him for good. The landscape hadn’t changed. It never did. But it felt smaller now. Not in size—just in silence.

He walked slowly, deliberately, circling the perimeter of his meager camp. The flicker of firelight was gone, only a wisp of smoke curling upward from the cave’s mouth. His boots crunched over the strange, shifting soil—something between ash and mineral, soft but sharp, like glass powdered by time. Every step unsettled something beneath it. Not life. Just layers of stillness.

He hadn’t realized how desperate he was to find *anything*—a trail, a mark, a remnant of another mind. The cave had given him artifacts. The dream had given him ghosts. Now he wanted *evidence*. Proof that this place wasn’t a prison built just for him.

At the far edge of the stone ridge, he paused. The terrain dipped slightly, curling inward where the slope caught wind and sediment. There, half-covered by a drift of fine grey, something caught the light—a flat surface, smooth and oddly uniform, too regular to be natural.

Bray dropped to one knee, swept it clean with both hands.

Stone. But not the same weathered shale that made up the surrounding ridges. This was darker, intentionally shaped, its edges beveled and worn smooth like the rim of a ritual basin. There was no name on it. Just lines—not carved deep, but shallow, careful, like someone had scratched them with a bone or a nail.

A map. Or the idea of one.

It wasn’t obvious. Not immediately. At first it seemed like random etching. But as Bray traced the lines, he felt them resolve, slowly, like his eyes had to relearn the rules of reading.

There were ridges, valleys, what might’ve been elevation markers. At the center of the etching was a series of boxed forms, arranged like walls stacked in layers. A fortress? A compound? Or something else entirely?

He kept following the lines. A faint path—perhaps a canyon or route—led from the area where he now stood toward that structure, though the path broke off midway and resumed only in dotted lines. Not a map made for clarity. A map made from memory.

In the upper right corner, someone had pressed down harder. Three slashed characters. Writing, he was almost sure, but not like the inscription in the cave. Those words had opened themselves to him. These stayed shut. Whoever had made them was not like him. Still, the shape felt… heavy. Like a mark made under strain, or left in warning.

Bray sat back, his knees popping with the motion.

This wasn’t left by accident. It wasn’t a message, not exactly. It was a record—or maybe a confession. Someone else had come this way. Someone who tried to make sense of the world by charting it with whatever they had left.

He didn’t know what that shape on the map represented. He didn’t know if it was hope or hazard. But it was *something*. In this world of stillness and dust and silence, it was the first sign of intent.

He stood, brushing stone dust from his palms. The sky above him remained unchanged—an endless, blank smear of bone-grey light. But for the first time, there was direction. Not a call. Not a promise.

Just a possibility.
